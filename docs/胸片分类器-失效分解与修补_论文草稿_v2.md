# Repairable and Irreducible Failures of Chest-Radiograph Classifiers: Operating-Point Collapse, Ranking-Layer Loss, and Evaluation Traps

**Authors:** Anonymous for review
**Draft status:** Revised from `胸片分类器-失效分解与修补_论文草稿_v1.pdf`
**Scope:** Cardiomegaly classification with a frozen DenseNet-121 U-MultiClass model

## Abstract

Chest-radiograph classifiers are commonly evaluated with AUROC and calibration metrics, while deployment requires a decision at a particular operating point. We study whether these two requirements fail in the same way under distribution and acquisition shift. A DenseNet-121 classifier trained on CheXpert is frozen and evaluated on a corrected patient-level CheXpert split, the official CheXpert validation set, NIH ChestX-ray14, and paired CheXphoto conditions containing clean, real re-photographed, and synthetic-degraded images. We decompose failure into a ranking layer, which orders cases by risk, and an operating-point layer, which maps scores to decisions. The two layers behave differently. AUROC remains 0.829 on the prior-shifted CheXpert split and 0.705 on NIH, while a fixed threshold under-calls by 4.4 times on one target and over-calls by 8.8 times on another. A threshold selected to match an estimated target prevalence repairs this operating-point error without target-image labels: on real CheXphoto re-photographs, decision cost falls from 0.6535 to 0.5050 and sensitivity rises from 0 to 0.4848. The repair does not recover ranking-layer information: the repaired cost remains 0.193 above the clean paired condition and the paired AUROC drop is 0.1696. Temperature scaling provides a counterexample to calibration-only model selection: ECE improves in every paired condition, but decision cost increases in three of four conditions and sensitivity remains zero on real re-photographs. Finally, paired experiments show that synthetic degradation is not a reliable proxy for real acquisition degradation: the same noise parameter gives AUROC 0.475 or 0.812 depending only on whether it is applied at the model input resolution or before a resize. These results support a conservative evaluation rule: report ranking, operating-point behavior, sensitivity, decision cost, and degeneracy baselines together, and treat image-quality signals as diagnostic evidence rather than as a validated routing policy.

## 1. Introduction

A chest-radiograph classifier can preserve useful ranking information while making clinically unusable decisions. AUROC measures whether positive cases tend to receive higher scores than negative cases, but deployment also requires a threshold, an expected case mix, and an error trade-off. A model can therefore have a respectable AUROC and still classify almost every image as negative or positive after a shift in prevalence or acquisition conditions.

This paper studies that distinction directly. We use one frozen Cardiomegaly classifier and evaluate it across source-like, prior-shifted, cross-institution, and real acquisition-shift conditions. The analysis is deliberately diagnostic: rather than proposing another routing architecture, we ask which failures can be repaired from the output scores and which require a better representation or new training data.

Our main finding is a two-layer decomposition. The operating point can move substantially even when ranking remains above chance. When the target prevalence is available or can be estimated without individual labels, a prevalence-matched threshold can restore a sensible decision rate. This intervention changes the threshold but not the ordering of images. The remaining gap after repair is therefore evidence of ranking-layer or representation loss, not a calibration problem.

The study also documents three evaluation traps encountered during development. First, ECE- or NLL-optimal temperature scaling can improve a calibration statistic while reducing sensitivity or selecting an effectively dead classifier. Second, a synthetic degradation parameter is not a model-level quantity unless the degradation and quality measurement are applied at the same resolution as the model input. Third, a soft-error target of the form `1 - p(y)` contains the model score by construction; evaluating score-correlated signals against it inflates apparent routing performance.

### Contributions

1. **A mechanism-level decomposition.** We separate operating-point collapse from ranking-layer loss and quantify both on the same frozen classifier across four evaluation settings.
2. **A label-free operating-point repair.** Matching the score threshold to an estimated target prevalence reduces decision cost without using target-image labels. We state the assumptions and the failure boundary explicitly: the repair is not valid when ranking has collapsed.
3. **An irreducible residual.** On paired CheXphoto images, operating-point repair leaves a 0.193 decision-cost gap and a 0.1696 paired AUROC loss between clean and real re-photographed images.
4. **Evaluation counterexamples.** We show that ECE can improve while decision quality worsens, and that resolution-inconsistent synthetic degradation and score-dependent soft-error targets can manufacture apparent quality-routing gains.

We do not claim that hand-crafted quality features constitute a validated clinical routing system. In our fixed-budget routing experiments, their incremental decision-cost benefit is small and unstable. Their defensible role in this paper is diagnostic: they help characterize failure strata and expose where the classifier's confidence has stopped being informative.

## 2. Related Work

The paper builds on four lines of work: external validation and dataset shift in medical imaging; post-hoc calibration; prior-shift correction; and selective prediction or reject-option classification. Existing work motivates each component, but our focus is the interaction between them. We compare a monotone score transformation with a threshold relocation under the same frozen model, and we use paired real-versus-synthetic images to test whether a robustness benchmark preserves the failure mode it claims to measure.

## 3. Data and Protocol

### 3.1 Evaluation settings

The main task is Cardiomegaly classification. The source model is trained on CheXpert using the U-MultiClass representation: negative, positive, and uncertain. Binary metrics exclude uncertain samples rather than treating them as negative. The four principal settings are:

| Setting | Images | Patients | Prevalence | Role |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---|
| CheXpert route-validation | 17,816 known-label images | patient-level split | 0.126 | source-like reference |
| CheXpert official-valid | 202 | 200 | 0.327 | same-source prior shift |
| NIH ChestX-ray14 test | 25,596 | 2,797 | 0.0418 | cross-institution and label-system shift |
| CheXphoto clean / re-photographed | 202 paired frontal images | 200 | 0.327 | real acquisition shift |

CheXphoto also supplies synthetic photographic and synthetic digital conditions for the same 202 images. These are paired conditions, not independent cohorts. NIH labels are extracted from reports by NLP, whereas CheXpert labels use a different labeling process. Therefore, NIH is an external stress test, not proof of label-measurement equivalence.

### 3.2 Patient-level split repair

An earlier implementation sorted patients by their majority label and then took global slices. This caused calibration and routing sets to have an artificially low positive prevalence. We replaced it with patient-level stratified random allocation into model-train, calibration, route-validation, and model-selection sets, while preserving the official validation patients. For the repaired seed=42 and seed=7 splits, all pairwise patient overlaps are zero and the mapped positive-rate deviation is below 1.5 percentage points. Results generated with the earlier split are retained as `invalid_split_diagnostic` and are not used for the main claims.

### 3.3 Model and frozen score

The primary classifier is a DenseNet-121 with a three-class head and 224×224 input. It is trained on the repaired CheXpert split and calibrated once on a disjoint calibration set. For the main four-domain analysis we use seed=42, with temperature `T = 0.494342`. The calibrated positive score is

\[
p(x) = \operatorname{softmax}(z(x)/T)_{\mathrm{positive}}.
\]

The score, model weights, and temperature are frozen for target-domain evaluation. ResNet-50, additional random seeds, and the original Agent routing experiments are reported as robustness and negative-result evidence, not as the proposed method.

### 3.4 Metrics and decision cost

We report AUROC for ranking, predicted positive rate (PPR), sensitivity (TPR), error rate, ECE, and an asymmetric decision cost

\[
C = \frac{2\,\mathrm{FN} + \mathrm{FP}}{N}.
\]

The ratio 2:1 is a study-level placeholder, not a clinical utility estimate. We always report TPR and PPR beside cost. The all-negative reference has cost `2π`, where `π` is target prevalence; this makes low-cost degenerate solutions visible.

All intervals for NIH and CheXphoto are patient-level cluster-bootstrap intervals. The CheXphoto paired comparisons resample patients, preserving the four conditions for each patient. ECE uses fixed-width bins and is interpreted descriptively rather than as a sufficient deployment objective.

### 3.5 Prevalence-matched threshold

Let \(\hat\pi_t\) be an estimate of target prevalence. The prevalence-matched threshold chooses the score quantile that predicts approximately \(\hat\pi_t\) positives. This is a threshold relocation, not a representation update and not a guarantee of cost optimality. It requires no target-image labels but does require a target-prevalence estimate. We also report a half-sample prevalence estimate in the source experiments to quantify estimation error.

## 4. Results

### 4.1 Ranking and operating-point behavior separate

With the frozen score and threshold 0.5, the classifier gives the following baseline:

| Setting | AUROC | Error rate | TPR | PPR | PPR / prevalence | ECE |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| CheXpert route-validation | 0.844 | 0.129 | 0.479 | 0.1235 | 0.98× | 0.022 |
| CheXpert official-valid | 0.829 | 0.262 | 0.212 | 0.0743 | 0.23× | 0.188 |
| NIH ChestX-ray14 | 0.705 | 0.357 | 0.618 | 0.3668 | 8.78× | 0.158 |
| CheXphoto re-photographed | 0.659 | 0.327 | 0.000 | 0.0000 | 0.00× | 0.237 |

The source-like route-validation split has a matched predicted positive rate because its case mix is close to the training case mix. The two shifted settings fail in opposite directions: the official CheXpert split under-calls by 4.4×, whereas NIH over-calls by 8.8×. Real re-photographed images preserve some ranking information but place every score below 0.5. These are different failure mechanisms and should not be summarized by AUROC alone.

### 4.2 Zero-label operating-point repair

Prevalence matching reduces decision cost in the shifted settings:

| Setting | Frozen threshold cost | Matched-threshold cost | TPR before → after |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| NIH ChestX-ray14 | 0.3728 | 0.1044 | 0.618 → 0.1665 |
| CheXphoto re-photographed | 0.6535 | 0.5050 | 0.000 → 0.4848 |
| CheXphoto clean | 0.5198 | 0.3119 | 0.212 → 0.6818 |
| CheXphoto synthetic photographic | 0.5594 | 0.3713 | 0.1515 → 0.6212 |
| CheXphoto synthetic digital | 0.4851 | 0.3564 | 0.2727 → 0.6364 |

The intervention does not improve ranking and is not universally cost-optimal. It corrects the score-to-decision mapping. On NIH, the lower TPR after repair is important: the method does not create sensitivity that the ranking layer does not contain. It removes the misleading appearance of a high-sensitivity, high-false-positive operating point and exposes the model's actual limit at the target prevalence.

### 4.3 The irreducible ranking-layer residual

CheXphoto provides the cleanest paired test. The same 202 images and labels are scored in every condition. Real re-photography lowers AUROC by `0.1696` with patient-level 95% CI `[-0.2564, -0.0967]`, while synthetic photographic and synthetic digital degradations lower AUROC by only `0.0155` and `0.0184`, respectively, with intervals crossing zero. After the matched-threshold repair, real re-photography still costs `0.5050`, compared with `0.3119` for the clean condition. The residual is approximately `0.193`.

No threshold relocation can remove a change in pairwise ordering. This residual therefore marks the boundary of score-only repair and motivates representation-level robustness work.

### 4.4 ECE is not decision quality

On the four paired CheXphoto conditions, an ECE-optimal temperature reduces ECE in every condition. However, decision cost rises in three conditions and remains unchanged in real re-photographs, where sensitivity stays at zero. On the real re-photographed condition, the ECE-optimal temperature is `T = 7.165`; it produces a smaller calibration error while leaving all images below the decision threshold. NLL optimization can produce an even lower numerical cost by approaching the all-negative predictor. Therefore a calibration table without TPR, PPR, cost, and the `2π` baseline is incomplete and can be actively misleading.

### 4.5 Synthetic degradation is not a real-degradation proxy

We compare two pipelines on the same images and weights. In design A, degradation is applied after resizing to the model input resolution (224×224). In design B, degradation is applied at 320×320 and then attenuated by resizing to 224×224. For noise with σ=0.10, AUROC is `0.4751` in design A and `0.8122` in design B, while `quality_risk` is `0.3750` and `0.3760`. The parameter σ therefore does not specify the model-level perturbation unless the resolution path is part of the definition.

The evaluation target can also be circular. A soft-error target `1 - p(y)` contains the model score. On the four CheXphoto conditions, AUROC for `p` predicting this target is `0.844–0.951`, exceeding the genuine candidate signals. The correct reference for any score-correlated signal is the score itself, and deployment-facing comparisons should use cost or risk-coverage metrics that do not define the target from the predictor.

### 4.6 Image-quality routing is diagnostic, not established as a method

On synthetic degradation strata, adding 17 quality features to confidence can increase error-detection AUROC, especially when confidence has collapsed; across 13 design-A conditions the gradient-boosting increment averages `+0.0330` and correlates with residual confidence at `r = -0.73`. NIH reproduces the negative relationship across multiple quality stratification axes. These findings support a conditional diagnostic statement: quality signals can be informative when the model's own confidence has failed.

They do not establish a deployable quality router. In six fixed-budget routing comparisons, five patient-level 95% intervals for the incremental decision-cost effect crossed zero, and the only interval excluding zero had an effect of about 0.14 percentage points. The primary conclusion is therefore not “quality-aware routing works,” but “quality features can describe failure regimes without reliably improving a fixed-budget decision policy.”

## 5. Discussion

The practical implication is to choose the intervention according to the failure layer. If ranking is intact and the target case mix has changed, estimate the target prevalence and relocate the threshold. If ranking has degraded, a threshold, intercept, or temperature cannot recover the lost ordering; the remedy must involve data, augmentation, architecture, or representation. If only ECE improves, do not infer that the clinical decision improved.

The experiments also change how synthetic robustness studies should be interpreted. A synthetic ladder is useful for controlled stress testing, but its parameters must be defined at the model input and its conclusions must be checked against paired real acquisition changes. Otherwise, the ladder can understate high-frequency degradation or measure a different image from the one the classifier sees.

The quality-routing experiments are retained as negative and boundary results. They show why the original Agent hypothesis was attractive and why it is not yet a defensible primary contribution. A quality feature may identify a population or regime in which confidence becomes unreliable, but that is weaker than reliably ranking individual cases for limited human review.

## 6. Limitations

First, the main task is one label, Cardiomegaly, and the principal model is one DenseNet-121 trained on CheXpert. The mechanism is plausible beyond this setting, but the paper does not establish architecture- or disease-universal behavior. Second, CheXphoto contains only 202 paired images and 200 patients, so paired estimates are informative but imprecise. Third, NIH labels are report-derived and not measurement-equivalent to CheXpert labels. Fourth, the FN:FP ratio of 2:1 is a placeholder and should be sensitivity-analysed before clinical interpretation. Fifth, the prevalence-matched threshold uses target prevalence; if prevalence is poorly estimated or if ranking fails, the repair can be misleading. Sixth, the NIH analysis relies on a third-party resized mirror and a patient-ID reconstruction audit; the original data provenance and mapping should be documented with the release used for a final paper. Finally, the TTA view budget and several diagnostic choices were explored on small cohorts and should not be presented as tuned clinical algorithms.

## 7. Conclusion

Chest-radiograph classifier failure is not a single calibration problem. Across source-like, prior-shifted, cross-institution, and real acquisition-shift conditions, ranking and operating-point behavior can fail independently. A target-prevalence-matched threshold is a simple, label-free way to repair the operating point when ranking remains useful. It cannot repair the ranking-layer loss exposed by paired real re-photography. Temperature scaling and ECE alone can select the wrong intervention, while resolution-inconsistent synthetic degradation and score-dependent soft-error targets can manufacture apparent routing success.

The resulting research direction is therefore a failure-analysis and repair framework, not a claim that a hand-built quality Agent solves deployment robustness. The next decisive experiment is a frozen external validation on an approved PhysioNet cohort, with patient-level uncertainty, fixed repair rules, and decision-cost reporting pre-specified before evaluation.
